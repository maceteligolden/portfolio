My website should communicate these things naturally, without sounding inflated, artificial, generic, or overly promotional.

## 3. How I became an engineer

My career did not begin with an impressive job title, a formal software engineering role, or a plan to become an AI engineer.

My dad introduced me to computers and Microsoft Office. My curiosity developed beyond the tasks I was initially shown. I wanted to understand what else a computer could do and how I could use it to create things.

I began experimenting with websites and online communities. One early project was a Wapka forum called "Wisdom into Bisi," built with Wisdom. It grew to approximately 1,000 users and was hacked more than once.

That experience gave me an early encounter with the realities of building something other people use. Creating a website was one thing; keeping it useful, accessible, and secure was another.

I went on to build WordPress websites, including an art e-commerce site associated with approximately $20,000 in transactions. Treat that figure carefully: do not describe it as my personal revenue, profit, or business earnings.

I experimented with Python, speech-to-text, and an early fixed-response chatbot. While studying Mechatronics Engineering, I also worked with JavaScript to build CMS features, ticketing systems, and internal tools.

My engineering degree brought together software, hardware, control systems, and computer vision. I worked on a drone-related project involving flight control and image analysis for agricultural and electrical inspection applications. The team won a state-level competition and placed fourth nationally.

My professional development then progressed through frontend engineering, freelance and client work, broader full-stack responsibilities, technical leadership, cloud architecture, and AI product development.

This progression matters. It explains why I care about more than writing code that works in isolation.

I have seen software from different perspectives: the person experimenting, the developer building a feature, the engineer maintaining a system, the technical lead coordinating work, and the product builder trying to solve a business problem.

Use this story selectively. The relationship with my dad and my early curiosity can add personality to the About page, but my entire website should not read like a childhood memoir. Connect personal history to the engineer I became.

Never embellish the story, manufacture emotional moments, or add dialogue and motivations that I have not provided.

Present me as someone who explores deeply and then applies that understanding, rather than someone who collects tools and trends.

Express this through examples of my work wherever possible. Do not simply call me a "systems thinker" without supporting evidence.

### Product-minded

I care about what software enables people to do.

A technically impressive implementation is not enough if the user cannot understand the result or act on it.

This is especially important in my AI work. I am interested in turning model outputs and complex information into useful decisions, not simply integrating an LLM and displaying its response.

When discussing my work, explain the problem, the engineering choices, and the practical purpose behind them.

### Comfortable with ownership

I have worked in environments where responsibilities extend beyond implementing an isolated ticket. My experience includes leading engineering work, making implementation decisions, working across the stack, and contributing to products from development through deployment.

Communicate ownership through concrete responsibilities.

Do not imply that I was the sole architect, sole developer, or final decision-maker for every project unless the evidence supports it.

### Calm and persistent when solving problems

I tend to work through technical difficulties systematically, investigate causes, simplify complex issues, and continue until I understand what is happening.

I enjoy architecture discussions, explaining technical concepts, mentoring others, and helping make difficult problems easier to reason about.

Avoid empty personality adjectives. Where appropriate, demonstrate these qualities through debugging stories, architecture decisions, collaboration, and technical explanations.

### Independent learner

My progression into AI product development has involved studying unfamiliar concepts and applying them in real systems.

Present me as capable of learning independently and applying new knowledge, while respecting the difference between having experimented with a technique and having extensive production experience with it.

## 5. My engineering philosophy

These principles should influence the language used across my website.

1. **Solve the actual problem.** Understand the underlying need before selecting a technology or proposing a solution.
2. **Prefer simplicity with sufficient structure.** Avoid unnecessary abstraction, orchestration, services, and complexity. Good architecture is appropriate to the problem, not architecture for its own sake.
3. **Make complexity understandable.** Technical systems should be explainable to the people who maintain them and usable by the people who depend on them.
4. **Design for real conditions.** Consider errors, retries, permissions, data quality, observability, deployment, and maintenance—not just the successful demo.
5. **Use AI purposefully.** An AI feature should improve a meaningful workflow or outcome. Model output alone is not proof of product value.
6. **Take the whole system seriously.** Interfaces, APIs, databases, infrastructure, and user experience are connected. Decisions in one layer affect the others.
7. **Keep learning through building.** Reading and experimentation matter, but applying knowledge to a real problem develops engineering judgement.
8. **Be honest about outcomes.** Explain what has been built, what has been tested, what is still being improved, and what has not yet been demonstrated.

Do not turn these principles into a preachy manifesto. Let them shape project descriptions and technical writing.

## 8. My voice and writing style

Write as a thoughtful engineer explaining real work to another intelligent person.

The voice should be:

- Direct rather than ceremonious.
- Confident without being boastful.
- Personal without oversharing.
- Technically informed without unnecessary jargon.
- Conversational without being casual to the point of losing professionalism.
- Specific rather than filled with general claims.
- Reflective when discussing lessons, trade-offs, or career development.
- Concise where the reader needs a quick understanding, and detailed where technical depth adds value.

Start with the point. The first one or two sentences should tell the reader something meaningful.

Prefer language like:

"I build software systems that connect application logic, infrastructure, and AI to solve practical problems."

Or:

"Detecting an anomaly is only the beginning. The harder product problem is helping someone understand what changed and decide what to do next."

These are examples of the desired style, not mandatory copy.

Avoid language like:

- "Passionate and results-driven technology enthusiast."
- "Dynamic, innovative, and highly motivated professional."
- "Leveraging cutting-edge technologies to drive digital transformation."
- "I am a rockstar engineer who thrives on disruption."
- "I have a proven track record of delivering world-class solutions."

Do not use buzzwords to manufacture seniority. Terms such as scalable, robust, enterprise-grade, production-ready, and high-performance should appear only when the engineering evidence supports them.

## 9. The difference between seniority and inflated language

I want the website to communicate my actual level of experience clearly.

Do not undersell me as a junior developer or generic aspiring AI engineer. I have substantial hands-on software engineering experience and technical leadership experience.

At the same time, do not automatically call me a principal engineer, staff engineer, AI researcher, machine-learning specialist, or cloud architect simply because I have worked across these areas.

Demonstrate senior-level qualities through:

- The problem and constraints I understood.
- The architecture and trade-offs I considered.
- The implementation responsibilities I owned.
- The failure cases and operational concerns I addressed.
- The collaboration and technical decisions involved.
- The outcome, when the outcome can be established.

Use situation → task → action → result as the default structure for substantive case studies.

For each story, answer:

1. What was the situation?
2. What problem or responsibility did I have?
3. What did I personally do?
4. Why did I choose that approach?
5. What challenges arose, and how were they addressed?
6. What changed as a result?
7. What evidence supports the result?
8. What did I learn, or what would I improve?

If the result is unknown, say what was implemented and leave the outcome unclaimed. An honest implementation story is better than a fabricated success story.

When a claim cannot be verified, inspect the available source material or omit it. Ask me only when the missing detail materially affects the accuracy of the page.

## 12. Rules for editing my existing portfolio

Before making changes:

1. Inspect the current website structure, pages, copy, project data, design system, and responsive behaviour.
2. Identify the existing claims about my background and compare them with available evidence.
3. Separate factual corrections from positioning improvements, copy improvements, and visual design changes.
4. Preserve useful content and working functionality.
5. Identify contradictions, unsupported claims, generic copy, repeated information, and missing proof.
6. Establish the desired narrative before rewriting individual sections.

When implementing:

- Maintain the existing framework and design conventions unless there is a concrete reason to change them.
- Keep the copy consistent across the homepage, About page, project pages, experience section, metadata, and downloadable CV.
- Preserve accessibility, responsiveness, performance, and maintainability.
- Avoid introducing unnecessary dependencies or elaborate components to solve simple presentation problems.
- Do not fabricate testimonials, company logos, client endorsements, project screenshots, or case-study results.
- Keep project descriptions consistent with the actual state of the code and product.
- Prefer a small number of well-developed case studies over many shallow project cards.
- Make changes incrementally and explain the purpose of significant content or structural changes.

Do not rewrite the entire website just because a different approach seems more exciting. Improve what exists when it serves the objective.

## 13. How to decide whether a change is good

Before accepting any major copy change, ask:

- Is this factually true?
- Does it sound like a real engineer rather than a marketing template?
- Does it communicate my actual experience level?
- Is it specific enough to distinguish me from other candidates?
- Does it explain what I did, rather than merely name a technology?
- Can I defend the claim in an interview?
- Does it help the intended reader understand my value?
- Is it concise enough to respect the reader's time?
- Does it match the rest of the website?

If a statement sounds impressive but cannot survive these questions, rewrite it.
